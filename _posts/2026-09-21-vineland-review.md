The seventh milestone in my personal Pynchonian pilgrimage, and, despite the, by now advanced, stage of my journey, the vanished author still eludes me.

Technology has always been one of the columns that support the temple of the Pynchon novel. In 1980s California, technology has just got its foot in the door as a tool of suppression. Its citizens have surrendered their attention to the TV for the last two decades; the ones that haven't succumbed to the tube are too unfamiliar with "the computer"--as most of the characters call the newborn head of the cybernetic hydra-- and as a result view it as some form of witchcraft aimed at their throats. The characters' relationship with technology is in stark contrast with the one present in Bleeding Edge, which was mostly positive, even empowering. In Vineland, the computer serves as an infallible well of data strictly at the hands of control hungry enforcers, while in the early 2000s NY from Bleeding Edge the computation that mattered was capillary; some nerd in his basement could come up with a million dollar idea, execute it, and immediately feel a white hand that smelled like money on their shoulder. Obviously, the surveillance was turned up a notch or two, but most people either accepted it as a constant in their life's formula, or were too distracted by the wonders of html to notice. I think that the wheel of time might have completed a full revolution at the turn of the 2020s; with the birth of the LLM, multinationals are hoarding all the silicon again, and their sights are set, as always, on government contracts.

The witty humor, pop references, and absurd characters I've come to expect from the author are here to stay; from a ninja sisterhood, to vampiric record producers, and karmic ghosts. Another staple of Pynchon's I've come to love is the unbelievable proficiency with which he can excavate the human heart. If our vital organ were an oil well, we would all get our pitchforks and march to stop him from fracking it. This time around, the experience of loss, either as a result of death, exile, or abandonment, are the spoils of this peculiar crime against the environment of man.
Prarie sets her eyes on her mother's for the first time at fourteen, at least since she can remember; the dead haunt northern California incapable of restoring the balance in their books of wrongs and rights. Prarie's mother, Frenesi, is the rock that shatters the revolutionary dream of the 60s, tossed by her lover's hand wearing the black leather glove of fascism, and is left to pick up the pieces. This last part is the highlight and backbone of the novel. Propelled by the brilliant framing device of Frenesi's film reels, reading the core of this novel felt like watching my favorite movie from back in the day.

Mason & Dixon is looking at me from my bookshelf as I write this. I think I just saw one of the two Os in the title on the spine wink at me! I'll see how many novels I can read before I give in to its flirting...
